Gameplay

Game Description

Room For One More is a cozy management puzzle where players arrange furniture to fulfill customer requests within a limited room. Each purchase and placement requires players to consider their remaining space, available budget, and room layout.

Core Loop

Review a Request → Buy and Arrange Furniture → Fulfill the Request → Repeat

Review the customer’s needs and check the available space and budget. Purchase furniture, rotate it, and find a suitable placement. Complete the request and prepare the room for the next customer.

Personal Contributions — Game Designer

I originated the concept in response to the “Limited Space” theme, taking inspiration from the room customization in PewDiePie’s Tuber Simulator. I wanted to explore room arrangement as the main gameplay activity, with limited space and money shaping the player’s decisions.

I designed the initial core loop: players receive a starting budget, respond to a grandmother’s requests, and purchase and arrange furniture to fulfill them. I also contributed ideas for furniture items.

Working alongside another designer, I co-developed the game flow, furniture database, and task structure. Our shared documentation covered furniture prices, dimensions, charisma, comfort, and functionality values, as well as task requirements and rewards.

During team discussions, I proposed restricting furniture sales per round to preserve the spatial challenge. I also proposed charisma-linked tips as an additional source of income. Tipping and a sales restriction were incorporated into the game, although the overall economy still needs further balancing.

Design Decisions — Preserving the Limited-Space Challenge

I raised a concern that unrestricted selling could weaken the game’s central constraint. If players could continually sell and replace furniture, they could work around the crowded room through repeated transactions, placing more emphasis on money management than on arranging a limited space.

I proposed limiting furniture sales per round so players would need to consider which items to keep and how to accommodate future requests. We discussed this as a team and incorporated a sales restriction into the game.

Design Decisions — Budget & Tipping

During development, we discussed how players should receive money and how that would affect the length and difficulty of a run.

I suggested exploring a larger starting budget and tips linked to charisma or customer satisfaction. This introduced another possible purpose for furniture choices beyond simply meeting a request: influencing the rewards a player could receive.

Tipping became part of the game, but its relationship with furniture costs, task rewards, and progression still requires balancing.

Collaborative Design Documentation

I worked with another designer to organize the game’s flow and initial design values. Our documentation included:

  • Game flow: Purchasing, arranging, submitting the room, and progressing between rounds.
  • Furniture data: Prices, dimensions, and charisma, comfort, and functionality values.
  • Task data: Furniture quantity requirements, stat thresholds, and monetary rewards.

These documents provided a shared starting point for implementation. The values represent our initial design rather than a fully balanced final system.

Design Breakdown — Space and Resource Balancing

The design combines two constraints: space and money. A piece of furniture may be affordable but difficult to fit, while a layout that satisfies one request may leave little flexibility for the next.

The balancing goal was to keep both constraints relevant. Furniture placement should require planning, while purchasing decisions should encourage players to consider what they can afford beyond the current request. Rotation, selling, and replacement provide ways to reconsider a layout as the room’s needs change.
